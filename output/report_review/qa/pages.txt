Part 1. Game Introduction 
1.1 Game Story 
Background 
Egg Press 3D is a cooperative journey in which two ducks carry a nest containing a fragile egg through 
a large kitchen and an outdoor obstacle course. Each player controls one duck. Their relative positions 
determine how the shared nest moves and tilts, so  progress depends on communication and careful 
movement. 
The first playable map is Kitchen_Map, with preparation counters, stove hazards, a seesaw crossing, 
cold storage, a pantry and a final patio. It combines traversal with pressure plates, a linked -lights 
puzzle and a symbol-code lock. The second map, MainMap_Online, uses outdoor platforms, bridges, 
obstacles, checkpoints and a finish area. 
Players and roles 
Two players connect as a host and a client. Both use the same duck player prefab and movement 
abilities. Each has a player name and an independently selected cosmetic item. The host starts the 
match and resolves shared gameplay state, but both players participate in carrying the nest. 
Tone and vibe 
The game uses cartoon ducks, colourful scenery and  a warm orange interface. Cracks, egg -break 
visuals, screen feedback and the movement of the nest communicate danger. Reaching a checkpoint 
gives the pair a shorter recovery route after failure.

High concept 
A 3D online co -op platformer for two players carrying one nest and egg. Relative movement, height 
differences, collisions and jump timing can destabilise the egg. Players must navigate the route, solve 
environmental puzzles and reach the level goal together. 
Core loop 
Menu and equipment selection → create or join a Relay room → host starts with two players → 
loading and preparation → carry the nest, solve puzzles and collect coins → checkpoint recovery after 
failure → level victory → next level, replay or return to menu. 
Design pillars 
• Shared carrying: the nest is positioned from both players’ carrying points. Distance constraints 
and the duck rig keep the pair visually connected to it. 
• Recoverable failure: egg breakage or a player entering a death zone restarts both players and 
the egg at the current checkpoint, or at the start if no checkpoint has been reached. 
• Readable feedback: the egg has discrete damage stages, with cracks and local screen 
feedback. Falling out of the nest is a separate risk from accumulating damage. 
• Personal expression: players buy cosmetic head items with coins, preview them in the menu 
and display their own selected item during online play.

Rules and objective 
• Carry the egg from the start of the level to the goal without destroying it. 
• The egg does not have a 100-point health bar or continuous speed, acceleration or squeeze 
damage. With progressive damage enabled, eligible damage events increment a counter. Nest 
collisions can either damage the egg or eject it, as decided by the host. 
• When the egg breaks, both players and the nest restart at the saved checkpoint with a reset 
egg. Entering a configured Dead zone also restarts the pair. There is no score deduction or 
limited-life counter in the current round logic. 
• A checkpoint can be reported by one player; the server validates the contact and safe respawn 
positions for both. In Kitchen_Map, a player or the nest entering the final trigger can activate 
victory. In MainMap_Online, NetworkNest checks whether the nest touches Win_Area. The 
code does not require both ducks and the egg to enter the goal simultaneously. 
Coins and progression 
Coins are the implemented reward currency. A valid online pickup is approved by the server, despawns 
the shared coin and adds one coin to each connected player’s local wallet. Coins buy cosmetic items 
in the menu shop. A time score, seed score, restart penalty, star rating and competitive leaderboard 
are not implemented. 
Tips for cooperation 
• Agree on a route and communicate before changing direction or stepping onto a narrow 
platform. Movement is relative to the local camera. 
• Keep the players at compatible heights and avoid extreme nest tilt. The egg can roll toward 
the lower edge and leave the nest when the release angle is exceeded. 
• Use jump timing deliberately. The cooperative jump detector pairs timestamps from different 
players; a matched pair releases the egg from assisted carrying. In the current maps this can 
push it sideways out of the nest.

Damage and release rules 
The server owns the egg simulation and decides damage. Progressive damage is enabled by overrides 
on the nest instances in both gameplay scenes. Each accepted damage event changes DamageCount; 
the implementation uses discrete contact events rather than continuous stress formulas. 
• First and second damage events: DamageCount increases to 1 and then 2; the visual shows 
increasing cracks while the phase remains Intact. 
• Third damage event: the egg enters Broken and raises the round-failure event. Duplicate 
contacts on the same object and contacts in the same physics step are filtered. 
• Nest impact: after preparation and cooldown checks, the host chooses between applying a 
damage increment and ejecting the egg. The shared nest prefab currently sets a 0.5 crack 
probability and a 0.75-second cooldown. 
• Release and landing: ReleaseCarry marks the egg to break at its next eligible environment 
collision, even if it has fewer than two prior damage increments. Nest and player colliders, 
triggers and Win_Area are excluded from ordinary environment damage checks. 
• Recovery: NetworkRoundManager responds to egg failure with a checkpoint restart. The egg 
lifecycle also breaks an actively simulated egg below world Y = −50 as a safety fallback.

Assisted carrying and free flight 
NestEggCarryPhysics simulates a bounded egg offset on the nest floor using gravity, a small inertial 
contribution and damping. While carried, the body is moved in the nest coordinate system. Excessive 
tilt or paired jump events release it to server -side Rigidbody motion. There is no simulated internal 
yolk, raw-to-boiled conversion, player-controlled squeeze or Q/E balancing mechanic. 
Egg state machine 
NestEggPhase defines Waiting, Intact and Broken. Waiting covers setup; reset activates Intact; 
breaking activates Broken; a round restart creates the next intact simulation state. DamageCount 1 
and 2 do not create separate Cracked or Critical states. Player movement separately uses an Idle/Walk 
state machine, with jump handled by the motor. 
Waiting → preparation/reset → Intact → break condition → Broken 
Broken → checkpoint restart/reset → Intact 
Any active phase → stop/wait for preparation → Waiting 
 
DamageCount within Intact: 0 → 1 → 2. 
The next accepted damage event, or an eligible landing after release, produces Broken.

than claiming that every class follows every principle.

SOLID principles 
S — Single Responsibility: KeyboardPlayerInput reads movement/jump input; PlayerCharacterM otor 
applies movement; PlayerAnimationPresenter controls animation. These responsibilities are 
separated from session setup and UI. 
 
O — Open/Closed: PlayerCharacterMotor accepts IPlayerMovementConstraint. The motor can use a 
different constraint implementation without changing its movement calculation. 
 
L — Liskov Substitution: OnlineIdleState and OnlineWalkState implement OnlinePlayerState. 
OnlinePlayerStateMachine calls the same Enter, Tick and Exit contract regardless of the active state.

I — Interface Segregation: ICoinWallet exposes the wallet operations needed by coin collection; 
IPlayerMovementConstraint exposes movement limiting; IPuzzleInteractable exposes puzzle 
activation. Consumers do not depend on a large all-purpose game manager. 
Example: Coin receives an ICoinWallet through Initialize; it calls AddCoin after collection. Puzzle 
controllers reference IPuzzleInteractable to activate their gate. 
D — Dependency Inversion: RelaySession receives IRelayRoomService in i ts constructor, and coin

Design patterns 
State pattern: OnlinePlayerStateMachine changes between OnlineIdleState and OnlineWalkState. 
Jump input is passed separately to the motor; the egg uses an enum lifecycle rather than the five-state 
class hierarchy proposed in the original report. 
The state-machine example is Assets/Scripts/Player/Movement/OnlinePlayerStateMachine.cs. 
ChangeState calls Exit on the previous state and Enter on the next state, while Tick delegates 
movement decisions to the current state.

Strategy-style composition: IPlayerMovementConstraint lets PlayerCharacterMotor delegate 
movement limits. CarryDistanceConstraint supplies the current carrying constraint. This separates 
movement integration from the rule that keeps two carriers within reach. 
Observer pattern: CurrencyManager raises OnCoinChanged after AddCoin or a successful SpendCoin. 
CoinUI subscribes, updates its TextMeshPro label and unsubscribes when destroyed. RoomLobbyView 
similarly listens to roster changes; equipment visuals listen to the equipped-ID NetworkVariable. 
Singleton usage: SaveSystem, CurrencyManager, PlayerInventory and CharacterEquipment provide 
shared access in the existing implementation. They simplify access but require correct scene lifetime 
and initialisation. No Object Pool or HazardBase Template Method is claimed for the current 
gameplay. 
Network synchronisation: player movement is owner-authoritative; shared nest/egg state, checkpoint 
decisions and round outcomes are server -controlled. NetworkVariables carry persistent state and 
RPCs carry requests/events. Nest snapshots include both nest and egg data, and the client uses 
prediction, reconciliation and presentation smoothing. 
Interface-based puzzle design: LinkedLightsPuzzle and SymbolCipherPuzzle use IPuzzleInteractable for 
the gate. Their plain C# rule classes can be tested without running a scene. EditMode test sources exist 
for LinkedLightsBoard and SymbolCipherLock. 
2.4 Online Session Flow 
• Create room: RelayRoomService signs in anonymously, allocates room for one joining client 
and obtains a Relay join code. RelaySession configures UnityTransport and starts the NGO 
host. The join code is returned by Relay; the project does not generate a fixed four-character 
Fusion room name. 


PAGE 26
Egg Press 3D – Final Project Report   |   Page 26 
• Join room: the other player enters the shared Relay code. The session joins through Unity 
Transport, and RelayRoomRoster maintains the two-player list and names. Invalid or 
unavailable rooms report an error through the session UI. 
• The current menu supports Create and Join by code. Automatic quick matching and skill-based 
matchmaking are not part of this implementation. 
• Start: only the host sees an enabled Start action when both connected players and roster 
entries are present. There is no separate Ready button required from each player. 
NetworkGameStarter synchronises the first map load and then spawns each player object with 
its owner and display name. 
• Disconnect or Quit: leaving gameplay ends the two-player session for the other participant as 
well. ReturnToMenuTransition performs the circular cover/loading effect and returns to 
Menu_Scene. Reconnection and host migration are not implemented. 
2.5 Shop and Equipment 
The shop uses the menu duck as the preview character. MenuShopTransition moves the camera 
closer and slides the item panel in from the right. ShopUI selects a slot and ShopPreview asks 
CharacterEquipment to display the corresponding prefab at HatAnchor. Previewing does not save a 
new equipped item. 
Buy calls ShopPurchaseService, which requests SpendCoin, appends the item ID to the owned list 
and saves it. SlotItem marks owned items and disables their button. CurrencyManager raises 
OnCoinChanged so subscribed coin displays refresh immediately. 
Outside the shop, PlayerEquipment cycles through owned IDs and an empty ID for no equipment. 
The chosen ID is saved. Closing the shop restores this saved selection. When an online player 
spawns, only its owner submits the saved ID; the server distributes that player’s ID and each 
machine creates the matching visual locally. 
SaveSystem stores coins using PlayerPrefs.SetInt and stores purchased IDs/equipped ID as JSON 
strings through JsonUtility and PlayerPrefs. These are local settings, not cloud/database records. The 
current network equipment check validates a known item ID, not a server-maintained purchase 
ledger.

2.6.6 Issues and Proposed Optimisation 
Draw Calls 
The earlier report records 4,725 batches for a gameplay capture. This is a useful optimisation lead, but 
the count depends on the scene, camera and render passes. It is not a universal limit or a current -
build measurement. 
A high batch count can increase render -thread submission cost. A zero “Saved by batching” statistic 
alone does not prove that every batching path is disabled; SRP Batcher behaviour must be inspected 
separately in the Frame Debugger. 
Proposed Solutions: 
● Evaluate static batching for suitable non-moving environment meshes, checking the memory 
cost and the render-pipeline configuration. 
● Evaluate GPU instancing for repeated compatible mesh/material combinations, and 
compare frame time before and after. Do not assume every repeated object can be reduced 
to a single draw call. 


PAGE 31
Egg Press 3D – Final Project Report   |   Page 31 
Geometry Workload 
The earlier capture reports approximately 9.4 million triangles and 9.8 million vertices. These ar e 
recorded rendering statistics and should be remeasured with the current content and camera views. 
High visible geometry can add GPU work. To establish the bottleneck, compare GPU timing and 
controlled changes to resolution, shadows and geometry. The FPS value and polygon count alone do 
not prove a GPU-bound frame. 
Proposed Solutions: 
● Review dense models and reduce detail that is not visible at gameplay distance. Preserve the 
silhouette and important visual features. 
● Evaluate LOD Groups for distant environment assets and verify transitions during actual 
camera movement. 
● Evaluate occlusion culling where level geometry can hide substantial content; measure its 
benefit against the cost and bake requirements. 
Shadow Workload 
The earlier report records 2,333 sha dow casters in the captured view. The number is historical and 
varies with scene visibility and shadow settings. 
Shadow passes render additional geometry from the light’s view. Shadow distance, cascades and the 
number of visible casters should be measured alongside GPU timing before selecting a change. 
Proposed Solutions: 
● Disable shadow casting on minor or distant decorative objects where it does not affect 
readability. 
● Evaluate baked or mixed lighting for static scenery while retaining suitable dynamic shadows 
for ducks, the nest and moving objects. Compare quality and performance rather than 
assuming this conversion is already complete.

Strengths 
• A clear and original mechanic: the shared fragile egg turns cooperation into the central 
challenge. 
• The project separates input, motor, animation, carrying and session concerns, and stores 
cosmetic catalogue data in ShopData. 
• Plain C# puzzle rules and small interfaces support focused testing; C# events and network 
state-change callbacks update the UI and replicated visuals. 
Limitations 
• Shared carrying remains sensitive to latency, jitter and packet loss. Prediction and smoothing 
are implemented, but no verified latency threshold is claimed without controlled multiplayer 
tests. 
• Authority is mixed: players drive their own movement, while the host resolves the nest/egg 
and shared outcomes. Cosmetic ownership and currency are stored locally, so this is not a 
server-secured economy. 
• Content consists of two gameplay maps. There is no reconnect/host-migration flow, automatic 
matchmaking, built-in voice chat, timed leaderboard or star scoring.

Lessons learned 
• Prototype the risky mechanic (shared physics) before building content and networking.

• More worlds: World 3 – Bathroom (soap, wet tiles), World 4 – Supermarket (conveyor belts,

the existing Unity networking stack. 
• Optional future work: introduce a timer, explicit scoring rules and leaderboards after designing 
and validating the score model.
